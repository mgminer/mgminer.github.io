**Publications**

* Marika Miner. 2025. “Strategic Shutdowns: The Subnational Targeting of Internet Shutdowns in India.” *Journal of Peace Research*.

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">Since 2016, governments in 77 countries have implemented 1,118 internet shutdowns, revealing a growing trend in the use of ‘digital repression.’ Governments often justify these shutdowns as necessary for public safety and applied impartially in response to security threats. Focusing on India, the country that has enacted the most shutdowns globally, this paper explores whether shutdowns instead follow a logic of targeting political opponents, and how they compare with traditional forms of physical repression. The results align with a theory of strategic political targeting as shutdowns are predominantly executed in areas with lower ruling party support, following opposition violence, and prior to escalations in military violence. This pattern differs significantly from that of physical repression, revealing that internet shutdowns are a unique and politically motivated tool used by those in power to suppress opposition and facilitate state-sanctioned violence.</p></details></div>

**Revise and Resubmit**

* Marika Miner. “Subnational Protest Diffusion: How the Farmers’ Protest Spread across India.” Revise and resubmit at *Journal of Peace Research*.

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">Protests often spread geographically across neighboring college campuses, towns, or countries. Scholars have debated whether this diffusion is simply due to nearby places experiencing common conditions that drive protest around the same time, or whether exposure to protest foments protest in nearby areas through a causal effect. If exposure has a causal effect, does geographic proximity exert an independent causal effect on protest diffusion, or does it capture underlying social linkages that constitute the true mechanisms of diffusion? This article examines how protests diffuse using observational analysis in the case of the India Farmers’ Protest (2020-2021) and a vignette experiment that holds exposure constant to isolate uptake, or how individuals respond once they learn of a protest. Both analyses show that while identity ties matter to some degree, geographic proximity is important in its own right, influencing protest by exposing people and making them more likely to react. Even in a highly digitally connected world, geography matters.</p></details></div>

* Jana Krause, Marika Miner, Melanie Sauter, and Imrana Buba. “Communal Conflict Research and Event Data Bias.” Revise and resubmit at *Journal of Peace Research*.

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">Communal violence is a pervasive yet theoretically underspecified form of political violence, prevalent across civil war contexts, electoral competition, and armed crime, and increasingly linked to climate-driven resource conflict. Despite advances in event data collection, measurement remains inconsistent — a problem rooted in conceptual ambiguity and the absence of standardized coding practices that hampers comparative and cumulative research. Drawing on a substantial engagement with the political violence literature, we propose a revised definition of communal violence centered on three core attributes: violence carried out by armed civilians who mobilize along a shared communal identity, with victims selected on the basis of social group membership. We show how this definition sharpens the conceptual boundaries that distinguish communal violence from civil war, electoral violence, and armed crime. We further introduce a novel, transparent, and adaptable keyword-based coding methodology grounded in fieldwork knowledge across multiple countries, which shifts focus from actor classifications to the defining features — practices, issues, and identities — of communal violence. We demonstrate the utility of these conceptual and methodological contributions through an analysis of communal and other forms of political violence in Nigeria, a country characterized by a complex conflict landscape in which communal violence intersects with insurgency, electoral violence, and banditry. Our findings advance the field of political violence research, with implications for the study of conflict, peacebuilding, and democratic governance, as well as climate change, adaptation, and social resilience.</p></details></div>

**Working Papers**

* Jana Krause, Marika Miner, and Imrana Buba. “Invisible Floods, Uncounted Victims: Data Limitations of Climate-Conflict Research in South Sudan.” Under review at *International Affairs*.

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">Climate change is widely regarded as a threat multiplier, yet research has produced inconsistent findings about its relationship with armed conflict. We argue that this inconsistency reflects not only the complexity of the climate-conflict relationship but also the uneven empirical foundations on which it is studied. Climate-conflict relationships are expected to be strongest in fragile settings, yet these are also where climate hazards, communal and other forms of armed violence, and the processes linking them are least comprehensively observed. We develop this argument through the case of South Sudan. A systematic review shows that climate-conflict research relies heavily on a small number of global datasets, making its empirical foundations highly sensitive to their strengths and limitations. Comparing the principal flood and conflict datasets reveals substantial inconsistencies in how flooding and communal violence are represented, with different dataset combinations producing markedly different empirical relationships, including null findings. Triangulating these data with humanitarian reporting, country-specific evidence, and qualitative fieldwork shows that global datasets capture only part of the flooding, communal violence, and the pathways linking them. We show that these limitations shape the evidence on which climate-conflict research and policy rely, underscoring the need for triangulation between global datasets and grounded evidence.</p></details></div>

* Jana Krause, Kathleen Klaus, and Marika Miner. “Inherently Vulnerable, yet Resilient Communities? Findings on Communal Violence and Local Peacebuilding in Kenya.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">How does exposure to recurrent, localized violence shape everyday social and political orientations? Research on the legacies of violence—largely focused on civil war—offers mixed expectations. Using original household survey data from two Kenyan counties with histories of communal violence, this study examines how exposure to violence is associated with both anti- and pro-social orientations. We find that exposure is linked to higher perceptions of insecurity, stronger resentment toward outgroups, and lower social trust, yet also to higher intergroup engagement. Together, these patterns show that recurrent violence reorganizes social life in uneven ways, combining fear, resentment, and continued engagement. We further show that local peacebuilding environments form part of the context through which exposure to violence is associated with different social and political orientations. The findings highlight the heterogeneous social legacies of recurrent violence and clarify how peacebuilding becomes embedded in everyday social life under persistent uncertainty.</p></details></div>

* Marika Miner and Lauren Young. “Incumbency Advantage in Violence: A Global Subnational Analysis.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">Since 2000, one third of all elections around the world have been affected by election-related violence. Agents of election violence are often rewarded with state patronage or forbearance, which is easier to access for incumbents. On the other hand, legislative incumbents may be less reliant on violence because they have access to other tools to win elections. Does holding a legislative seat increase or decrease the incidence of electoral violence perpetrated by that party? Does the effect of legislative incumbency differ across contexts? We assess the external validity of existing analyses of the effects of local incumbency on pre-election violence by creating a global subnational dataset of constituency-level election results, pre-election violence events, and other characteristics at the local, party, and national levels. We use a close-election regression discontinuity design to test for the effect of legislative incumbency in different types of contexts. Our data shows that local incumbent parties perpetrate more election violence. However, we do not find a significant relationship between legislative incumbency and electoral violence.</p></details></div>

* Marika Miner. “What Happens in the Dark? Evaluating the Use and Impact of Internet Shutdowns during African Elections.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">The digital era has ushered in sophisticated forms of political control, notably through shutting down the internet during pivotal moments. This phenomenon has become increasingly prevalent during elections in African countries, raising significant concerns regarding its implications for democratic processes. This paper explores the use and consequences of these shutdowns, highlighting empirical patterns on predictors of election-related shutdowns, taking into account country and election-specific contexts to understand what is driving the implementation of these shutdowns. It also assesses the electoral impacts of shutdowns, specifically their effects on voter turnout and election outcomes. This paper situates these findings in the broader context of electoral repression, offering a theoretical framework to understand the trade-offs and potential interplay between the use of digital tactics and more traditional forms of electoral repression such as political violence and electoral fraud.</p></details></div>

* Jana Krause and Marika Miner. “Compounding Vulnerability: Communal Violence and Floods in South Sudan.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">How do the effects of climate change impact dynamics of communal violence in South Sudan? The world’s youngest country has suffered from high levels of armed conflict, including protracted communal wars, for decades. The legacies of civil war and communal violence have resulted in profound instability, governance failure, and a dire humanitarian situation. South Sudan also ranks among the countries most vulnerable to the effects of climate change. Its population has suffered from rising temperatures, droughts, and floods. Since 2019, extreme floods have devastated large parts, resulting in widespread displacement, destruction of livelihoods, and agricultural produce. We analyze the vicious circle of communal violence and flooding in South Sudan. Rather than estimating a general effect of climate change on communal violence, we demonstrate how communal violence and flooding interlink and exponentially increase the population’s vulnerability to further armed conflict and future climatic hazards.</p></details></div>

* Marika Miner and Pengshan Pan. “Seeing the State Through Infrastructure: Chinese Road and National Identity in Kyrgyzstan.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">This study explores the effects of the North-South Transportation Corridor, a mountain road construction project in Kyrgyzstan, implemented under China’s Belt and Road Initiative (BRI). Utilizing a difference-in-differences design, this research illustrates the project’s role as an infrastructural link that bridges communities located across Kyrgyzstan’s diverse landscape, unifying major cities and traditionally isolated mountainous regions. The results show that this foreign-invested infrastructure development project inadvertently strengthened state capacity and national unity, with evidence of increased local income, enhanced trust in the central government, and improved inter-ethnic relations in households located within 10-25km from the project. On the other hand, we find decreased trust in local community leaders, along with shifting views on religious groups and diminished tribal identity among affected residents. Moreover, our analysis reveals significant heterogeneity in the impacts of the project, with more pronounced effects among historically marginalized tribes and particular ethnic groups.</p></details></div>

* Zeev Maoz, Brandon Kinne, Kai-Yin Lin, Stephanie Kang, and Marika Miner. “A Network Model of International Security Cooperation.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">We develop a dynamic network model of international security cooperation, which assumes that the international system is composed of actors with a wide array of attributes and interests. The model focuses on four sets of factors that drive the evolution of security cooperation. (a) Need reflects the motivation to form cooperative ties. (b) Attraction defines the relative attractiveness of prospective partners. (c) Prevention focuses on partner selection designed to prevent them from cooperating with enemies. (d) Trust reflects beliefs about partner’s reliability. We test the hypotheses deduced from this model on international alliances over the period of 1816-2014 and on defence cooperation agreements over the period of 1980-2010. We report four key findings. First, states select allies on the basis of a well-defined utility function that combines strategic, political, and cultural affinities. In addition, such utility functions include assessment of prospective allies’ reliability and potential costs associated with security cooperation. Second, states “quantitatively” overbalance - they form alliances whose capability pools significantly outweigh those of their enemies. By contrast, we find that states “qualitatively balance:” the capabilities of a state’s allies weighted by the utility it assigns to these allies produces a closer balance to that of its enemies. Third, the emergent results of this process forms significantly clustered, modular, and structurally balanced security cooperation networks. Fourth, our model helps explain the persistence of large and asymmetric alliances, key puzzles in the study of security cooperation.</p></details></div>

* Zeev Maoz, Kai-Yin Lin, and Marika Miner. “A Model of Burden Sharing in Security Cooperation Networks.”

  <div><details><summary style="cursor:pointer;">Abstract</summary><p style="margin-top:0.5rem;">We develop a network model of burden sharing in security cooperation ventures. States are rational agents who enter into security cooperation agreements - formal military alliances, defense cooperation agreements - in order to boost their security. We distinguish between burden sharing and compliance. Burden sharing means contributing to the common security goal of the agreement, whether or not the circumstances for invoking commitments arise. Compliance refers to fulfilment of one’s commitment according to the agreement’s terms. We then offer a model of the circumstances under which participants in security cooperation agreements are willing to contribute to the common goals of members. This model focuses on several factors. Need refers to the extent to which an agent needs such agreements to boost its security. Detection refers to the probability that partners to such agreement would detect free-riding by a member. Punishment refers to the probability and magnitude of sanctions against free-riders. Emulation reflects a tendency by actors to mimic partners’ burden-sharing behavior. Finally Fatigue reflects a growing tendency to free-ride as security agreements mature. We develop an agent-based model that (a) specifies the microfoundations of burden sharing in security cooperation agreements, (b) examines patterns of burden sharing, and (c) explores the network implications of these processes.</p></details></div>
